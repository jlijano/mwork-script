# MWORK Call Script Masterclass

## 1. What the Script Really Is

The MWORK call script is best understood as a **collision-repair customer acquisition and claim-navigation playbook**.

It is not merely a telephone script. The source combines several distinct functions:

1. live-call wording,
2. call-control guidance,
3. empathy and rapport coaching,
4. customer qualification,
5. insurance-path identification,
6. vehicle safety and driveability screening,
7. repair-facility positioning,
8. MWORK benefit and credibility messaging,
9. objection interpretation and rebuttal language,
10. voicemail scripting,
11. closing concepts,
12. and operational notes for what should happen next.

Because all of those functions are mixed into one large source, the material is rich but difficult to use in real time. The purpose of this masterclass is to separate the logic from the wording and explain what each part is actually trying to accomplish.

---

# 2. The Strategic Objective

The script is designed to move an accident lead through the following transformation:

**Unknown caller / uncertain accident situation**

→ understands why MWORK is calling

→ feels heard and supported

→ explains current claim status

→ confirms who controls the repair decision

→ determines whether the vehicle is safe and driveable

→ understands MWORK's independent-repair positioning

→ sees a relevant reason to work with MWORK

→ resolves the main objection

→ agrees to a concrete next action.

The call should therefore be judged not by how much of the script the agent reads, but by whether the agent successfully identifies the customer's situation and advances the claim or repair process.

---

# 3. The Core Call Architecture

The full call can be simplified into ten stages:

## CONNECT
Identify the customer, introduce yourself, reference the vehicle, and explain the practical reason for the call.

## CARE
Acknowledge the accident and show genuine concern for the customer's immediate situation.

## DISCOVER
Understand what has already happened with the vehicle, insurance, rental, towing, repair selection, and documentation.

## QUALIFY
Confirm the decision-maker and determine who is paying for the repair.

## SAFETY
Determine whether the vehicle is safe and driveable.

## POSITION
Explain who MWORK is, what being independent means, and how MWORK works with insurance.

## SOLVE
Connect a customer need to a relevant MWORK capability or benefit.

## HANDLE
Resolve objections by identifying the actual concern beneath the words.

## CLOSE
Secure the next action.

## DOCUMENT
Record the operational facts needed for follow-up, dispatch, estimating, rental, or future outreach.

This framework should be the backbone of training, QA, CRM design, and future script rewrites.

---

# 4. Stage One — CONNECT

The source opener references the customer's year, make, and model and immediately gives a practical reason for the outreach: MWORK may be able to help arrange inspection and rental if insurance has not already done so.

This approach is strategically sound because it answers the customer's first hidden question:

**“Why are you calling me?”**

A weak opening would begin with company history or a long explanation.

A stronger opening does four things quickly:

- confirms the right person,
- identifies the caller,
- anchors the call to the actual vehicle,
- and gives the customer a reason to continue listening.

### Desired effect

The customer should understand within seconds that the call is related to their recent accident and that the caller may be able to help with the next steps.

### Common failure

Agents often over-explain before establishing relevance. That increases suspicion and produces early objections such as:

- “Who are you?”
- “Are you with my insurance?”
- “How did you get my number?”

The opener must be brief enough that the customer can interrupt and still understand the purpose.

---

# 5. Stage Two — CARE

The script instructs the agent to ask how the customer is doing and to listen carefully for immediate needs. It also suggests acknowledging the accident and expressing concern for everyone involved.

This is not filler.

Accident leads are different from ordinary commercial leads. The person may be dealing with:

- pain or injury,
- family concerns,
- transportation problems,
- towing and storage,
- confusion with insurance,
- police reports,
- work disruption,
- financial anxiety,
- or repeated calls from multiple parties.

The empathy stage gives the customer space to reveal the problem that matters most to them.

### Important coaching principle

Empathy must be **responsive**, not theatrical.

The agent should not mechanically say “I’m sorry to hear that” regardless of what the customer said.

The source even distinguishes between positive and negative responses, suggesting language such as “I’m happy to hear that” or “I’m sorry to hear that.”

The real skill is listening.

### Better label

The source uses language similar to “establishing control.” Operationally, this means the agent should provide structure and direction. For training, a better label is:

**Establish Rapport and Conversation Leadership.**

The goal is not to dominate the customer. The goal is to make the conversation feel organized and helpful.

---

# 6. Stage Three — DISCOVER

The script contains a large bank of directional questions. These include questions about:

- where the vehicle is now,
- whether a claim has been filed,
- whether insurance has contacted the customer,
- whether a rental has been arranged,
- whether the customer has another vehicle,
- whether the customer has chosen a repair shop,
- whether a crash report is available,
- whether child car seats were in the vehicle,
- and whether the customer has been through an accident before.

These questions should not be treated as a checklist that must always be read in full.

They should be arranged in a logical discovery sequence.

## Recommended discovery order

### A. Vehicle
- Where is the vehicle now?
- Is it driveable?
- Was it towed?

### B. Claim
- Has a claim been filed?
- Which insurance company is handling it?
- Has an adjuster contacted you?

### C. Transportation
- Do you have another vehicle?
- Has a rental been arranged?

### D. Repair decision
- Have you selected a repair facility?
- Has the vehicle already been dropped at another shop?

### E. Documentation
- Do you have the crash report?
- Is there anything still missing from the claim?

This order moves from the customer's immediate physical problem to the administrative problem.

---

# 7. Stage Four — QUALIFY

The script asks whether the person on the phone is the person handling the repairs, working with insurance, and deciding where the vehicle will be repaired.

That is an authority check.

Before spending time on positioning and objection handling, the agent should know whether they are speaking with the actual decision-maker.

## Decision-maker outcomes

- **Yes** — continue.
- **No** — identify the correct person.
- **Shared decision** — identify who else is involved.
- **Unclear** — avoid forcing a close until authority is understood.

The script also requires the agent to determine who is paying.

The main pathways are:

### Own-policy claim
The customer is using their own collision or applicable coverage.

### Third-party claim
The customer is seeking payment through the other driver's carrier.

### Self-pay
The repair is being handled without an insurance claim.

### Undetermined
Liability, coverage, or claim status has not yet been established.

This branch matters because deductible, rental, liability, timing, and customer expectations may differ.

---

# 8. Stage Five — SAFETY AND DRIVEABILITY

The driveability section is one of the most operationally important parts of the source.

The script asks whether the vehicle is driveable and safe to be on the road, then provides questions covering conditions such as:

- deployed airbags,
- broken windows, mirrors, or glass,
- damaged headlights or taillights,
- fluid leaks,
- unsafe wheels or tires,
- new warning lights,
- hanging or damaged exhaust,
- pulling to one side,
- hood problems,
- door problems,
- trunk or liftgate problems.

These questions serve two functions:

## Customer safety
A damaged vehicle should not be casually treated as safe simply because it still moves.

## Operational routing
The answer determines whether the next step should be:

- towing or pickup,
- a drive-in inspection,
- an estimate appointment,
- or further evaluation.

### Critical ambiguity in the source

The source includes the phrase **“SKIP SAFETY QUESTIONS”** near the safety checklist.

That wording is dangerous because it is unclear whether it means:

- skip the questions when the vehicle is already obviously non-driveable,
- skip them in a specific severity category,
- or something else.

This should be clarified before training agents.

A safer workflow label would be:

**If the vehicle is already confirmed non-driveable/towed, do not unnecessarily repeat the driveability checklist. If driveability is uncertain, ask the safety questions.**

That interpretation should still be formally approved by MWORK leadership before being adopted as policy.

---

# 9. Stage Six — POSITION MWORK

The central positioning statement in the source is that MWORK is an **independent repair facility** that works directly with insurance companies for estimates and payments.

The script also emphasizes that the customer can choose the repair facility.

The strategic problem the script is trying to solve is a customer misconception:

**“If I do not use the insurer's preferred shop, I will be on my own.”**

MWORK's positioning attempts to separate two concepts:

- independence from an insurer's repair network,
- ability to work with insurers operationally.

That is the foundation of the sales message.

### Strong positioning sequence

Instead of delivering one long paragraph, agents should separate the ideas:

1. **Identity** — MWORK is an independent collision-repair facility.
2. **Coordination** — MWORK works with insurance companies on estimates and payments.
3. **Choice** — the customer is not being told that they must repair at one particular facility.
4. **Value** — MWORK can help coordinate the repair process.

This is easier to understand than stacking every pricing, insurance, and independence claim into one sentence.

# 10. Stage Seven — SOLVE WITH RELEVANT BENEFITS

The source contains a large MWORK benefit library. Examples include:

- lifetime repair/workmanship warranty concepts,
- full-service collision repair,
- bodywork,
- paint,
- frame work,
- mechanical work,
- suspension work,
- manufacturer certifications,
- I-CAR-related credentials,
- multiple Ohio locations,
- towing,
- rental support,
- deductible-assistance concepts,
- total-loss assistance,
- buyback-related concepts,
- OEM/genuine-parts positioning,
- and financing or payment-plan concepts for some customers.

This is useful knowledge, but it should not become a feature dump.

## The correct model is:

**Problem → Relevant Benefit → Customer Meaning → Next Question**

### Example: transportation problem

Customer: “I don’t have anything else to drive.”

Agent logic: rental support is relevant.

### Example: trust problem

Customer: “I’ve never heard of your shop.”

Agent logic: credibility, locations, certifications, warranty, and years in business are relevant.

### Example: repair-quality concern

Customer: “I want this fixed correctly.”

Agent logic: certified repair capability, full-service repair, manufacturer procedures, and warranty are relevant.

### Example: convenience concern

Customer: “I don’t want to deal with moving the car.”

Agent logic: towing/transport and coordination are relevant.

### Coaching rule

An agent should normally present **one or two highly relevant benefits**, not eight generic benefits.

Relevance is more persuasive than volume.

# 11. Credibility Stack

The source includes several proof elements designed to reduce trust resistance.

These include references to:

- lifetime warranty,
- full-service capability,
- I-CAR-related status,
- ASA certification,
- multiple locations in Ohio,
- years in business,
- manufacturer certifications,
- specialization or equipment for specific makes.

These are not merely features. They answer hidden customer questions:

- Is this company legitimate?
- Can they actually repair my vehicle?
- Will they stand behind the work?
- Are they experienced?
- Are they recognized by manufacturers or industry organizations?

### Training principle

The proof point must match the doubt.

Do not recite the entire credibility stack every time.

---

# 12. Early Interruptions and Trust Resistance

The source anticipates early customer interruptions such as:

- “Who told you to call me?”
- “Are you with my insurance company?”
- “Did insurance tell you to call?”
- “How did you get my information?”
- “Who are you?”
- “Why are you calling?”

The script's response pattern is generally:

1. identify MWORK,
2. explain the reason for the outreach,
3. explain the source of the information,
4. redirect to something useful for the customer.

This is the right overall pattern because arguing about the interruption makes the call feel defensive.

### Best-practice structure

**Answer → reassure → redirect.**

Example logic:

- answer the question directly,
- explain that the call is related to the accident/vehicle and MWORK's availability,
- then return to whether inspection, rental, towing, or repair selection has already been handled.

The customer should never feel that the agent is intentionally avoiding the question.

# 13. Objection Handling — The Strongest Training Section

The source objection table is built around four concepts:

**Objection → What it really means → Response → Close / return to question**

That is much stronger than teaching agents to memorize rebuttals.

The source recognizes that customer words often represent a deeper concern.

## Common underlying concerns

### Authority reliance
“My insurance told me to use their shop.”

### Convenience
“They said it will be faster.”

### Fear of cost
“I don’t want to pay anything out of pocket.”

### Fear of losing coverage
“They said they can’t guarantee it if I don’t use their shop.”

### Perceived commitment
“I already have an estimate.”

### Complexity avoidance
“I just want to go through insurance.”

### Delay / uncertainty
“I’ll wait to see what insurance says.”

### Commitment inertia
“I already dropped the vehicle at their shop.”

### Trust barrier
“I’ve never heard of your shop.”

### Passive decision-making
“I’ll just do what insurance recommends.”

### Logistics concern
“Will this delay my rental?”

### Switching friction
“I don’t want to switch shops.”

### Soft rejection
“I’ll think about it.”

### Unexpected-cost fear
“My insurance said I might owe money if I use your shop.”

This psychological categorization is valuable because it teaches agents what to solve.

---

# 14. A Better Objection Model: ACAEQ

The source contains strong rebuttal material, but many objections would benefit from a more explicit bridge back into discovery.

Recommended framework:

## A — Acknowledge
Show that you heard the concern.

## C — Clarify
Make sure you understand what the customer actually means.

## A — Answer
Provide the relevant information.

## E — Evidence
Use the most relevant MWORK proof point if needed.

## Q — Question
Move the conversation forward with a useful next question.

### Example

Customer: “I’ll just wait for insurance.”

Acknowledge: “That makes sense.”

Clarify: “Have they already assigned an adjuster?”

Answer: Explain the relevant next step without overloading the customer.

Evidence: Explain how MWORK coordinates with insurers if relevant.

Question: “Has anyone already scheduled the vehicle inspection?”

The final question is critical. A rebuttal without a bridge often ends the momentum of the call.

# 15. FEEL–FELT–FOUND

The source references the traditional sales method **FEEL FELT FOUND**.

Traditional form:

- I understand how you feel.
- Other customers have felt the same way.
- What they found was...

The framework can work, but agents who repeat it mechanically sound scripted.

A more natural version for MWORK is:

**Acknowledge → Normalize → Explain**

Example:

“I understand why you’d want to wait for insurance. A lot of people initially assume the insurer also chooses the repair shop. What we normally explain is how the repair-facility decision and the insurance claim process work separately.”

The intent of the original framework is preserved without sounding like a sales formula.

---

# 16. IF-I-COULD-WOULD-YOU

The source also references **IF I COULD, WOULD YOU?**

This is a conditional close.

Example structure:

“If I could help arrange the inspection and transportation, would you be comfortable getting the process started?”

This should only be used after identifying the customer's real obstacle.

It is strongest when the condition directly addresses the customer's stated concern.

Weak use:

“If I could do something for you, would you use us?”

Strong use:

“If we can coordinate the tow and work directly with the carrier on the inspection, would that solve the main issue you’re dealing with right now?”

# 17. Driveable vs. Non-Driveable Workflow

The source clearly intends two different operational paths.

## Non-driveable path

Likely next actions include:

- arrange tow or pickup,
- confirm vehicle location,
- coordinate insurance inspection,
- discuss rental/transportation,
- explain what happens if the insurer later declares a total loss.

## Driveable path

Likely next actions include:

- schedule inspection or estimate,
- confirm safe transport,
- determine rental/alternate transportation needs,
- coordinate with the claim process.

This branch should be visible in every live-call guide and CRM.

---

# 18. Insurance Path Logic

The source distinguishes between different claim situations.

## Own-policy claim

The customer may have a deductible depending on coverage and circumstances.

## Third-party claim

The source discusses the other carrier accepting or establishing liability and suggests that no deductible applies in certain claimant scenarios.

## Uninsured/other scenarios

The source also references uninsured-motorist and liability-investigation situations.

### Important caution

Insurance rules, deductibles, liability timelines, rental coverage, and payment responsibilities can vary by policy, facts, carrier, and jurisdiction.

Agents should not convert generalized training notes into unconditional promises.

Where the source states exact timeframes or absolute coverage outcomes, those statements should be formally reviewed before being treated as required language.

---

# 19. Independent-Shop Positioning

A major theme in the source is the contrast between an independent repair facility and a direct-repair-program or insurer-affiliated shop.

The source argues that independence allows MWORK to prioritize the customer and repair process rather than an insurer's contractual repair-network requirements.

It also emphasizes manufacturer repair standards, transparency, and repair quality.

This is central to the sales narrative.

### Training caution

Agents should explain MWORK's independent value without making unsupported generalizations about the quality, motives, or behavior of every insurer-recommended or DRP shop.

The strongest version of the message is positive and customer-centered:

- MWORK is independent.
- MWORK works with insurers.
- MWORK focuses on the repair and customer experience.
- MWORK can explain options and coordinate the process.

That is stronger than attacking competitors.

---

# 20. The Close — The Least Developed Part of the Source

The source contains extensive discovery, benefits, insurance information, safety logic, and objection handling.

The closing section is comparatively underdeveloped.

This is the largest structural gap.

A successful call needs a clearly defined next action.

## Recommended primary outcomes

### Inspection scheduled
Customer agrees to bring the vehicle or have it inspected.

### Tow / pickup scheduled
Vehicle is non-driveable or unsafe.

### Rental coordination started
Transportation is a major need.

### Documents requested or exchanged
Claim number, crash report, estimate, or other required information is needed.

### Follow-up scheduled
Customer is not ready but agrees to a specific callback.

### Already committed elsewhere
Record status and next possible follow-up.

### Declined
Respect the decision and document the disposition.

### Total-loss path
Repair sale may no longer be the main objective; follow the approved total-loss or buyback process.

The close should be operational, not vague.

Weak close:

“Let us know if you need anything.”

Strong close:

“Let’s schedule the inspection. Is morning or afternoon better?”

# 21. Why the Script Feels Difficult Today

The script's main problem is not lack of content.

It is **information architecture**.

The source simultaneously acts as:

- script,
- SOP,
- training guide,
- objection handbook,
- benefit catalog,
- insurance primer,
- safety checklist,
- and coaching document.

An agent cannot efficiently navigate all of that while speaking to a customer.

The solution is separation by use case.

## Recommended documentation system

### Live Call Flow
Only the questions and decision branches needed during a call.

### Objection Guide
Customer objection, underlying concern, recommended response logic, and bridge question.

### Knowledge Base
Certifications, services, programs, locations, insurance explanations, benefits, warranty, towing, rental, total loss, financing, and policy-approved statements.

### QA Scorecard
What good execution looks like.

### Coaching Guide
How managers diagnose agent performance.

This repository follows that model.

# 22. Internal Contradictions and Items Requiring Clarification

Several items should be clarified before the script is treated as a final controlled SOP.

## Safety wording
“SKIP SAFETY QUESTIONS” conflicts with the adjacent safety checklist.

## Quality vs speed
One part promotes “Quality over Speed,” while another promotes fast turnaround.

These can coexist, but the approved message should be something like:

**Efficient repairs without sacrificing repair quality.**

## Warranty terminology
The source references lifetime warranty language and also notes that the warranty does not transfer when ownership changes.

The exact scope and exclusions should be controlled by the official warranty terms.

## OEM/genuine parts
The source includes strong statements that MWORK uses genuine/original parts and not aftermarket parts.

That should match actual repair policy, insurer approvals, availability, customer authorization, and manufacturer requirements.

## Deductible assistance
The source references assistance up to $500 based on damage or program qualification.

Eligibility, limits, and legal/compliance requirements should be explicitly documented.

## Insurance-payment promises
The source contains broad “no money out of pocket” positioning.

That should be carefully qualified according to deductible, policy coverage, authorization, non-covered operations, betterment, customer-selected upgrades, storage, and other possible variables.

## Exact timeframes
The source includes timing expectations for pickup, inspections, and liability activity.

These should be framed as estimates unless MWORK can operationally guarantee them.

# 23. Recommended Agent Mental Model

Agents should not think:

**“What line comes next?”**

They should think:

1. Who am I speaking with?
2. What has already happened?
3. What is the customer's main problem?
4. Who is paying?
5. Is the vehicle safe?
6. Has a repair facility been chosen?
7. What is stopping the customer from moving forward?
8. Which MWORK capability solves that problem?
9. What is the next action?

That mental model creates natural conversations.

---

# 24. Recommended Call Philosophy

## Be conversational, not robotic
The source itself says to practice until natural.

## Ask before telling
Discovery should come before a large company pitch.

## Match value to need
Do not list benefits that have nothing to do with the customer's concern.

## Lead the process
Customers may not know the next step after an accident. The agent should provide structure.

## Do not overpromise
Insurance, repair timing, cost, and coverage statements must stay within approved language.

## Respect the customer's choice
The goal is to earn the next step, not pressure the customer into one.

## Close every call operationally
Every call should end with a documented next action or disposition.

# 26. Final Interpretation

The MWORK script is strongest when treated as a **conversation operating system**, not a giant script.

Its biggest strengths are:

- empathy,
- accident-specific discovery,
- insurance-path logic,
- driveability and safety qualification,
- strong independent-shop positioning,
- a broad value library,
- and sophisticated objection categorization.

Its biggest weaknesses are:

- excessive density,
- repetition,
- several absolute claims,
- ambiguous instructions,
- and an underdeveloped closing framework.

The best evolution of the script is therefore not to add more paragraphs.

It is to make the logic clearer.

The target operating model is:

**CONNECT → CARE → DISCOVER → QUALIFY → SAFETY → POSITION → SOLVE → HANDLE → CLOSE → DOCUMENT**

When agents understand that sequence, the original script becomes much easier to use, coach, measure, and improve.
